Understanding Family Courts

The family court system, though varying state to state, is argued to be "what is best for the child." Example: In Kansas, there is a statute, K.S.A. § 23‑3315, that explicitly says:

"Best interest of a child."
for visitations of a non-parent. Family courts differ from criminal courts, which are far more experienced and structured, so pseudoscience is not taken as evidence.

1. There is no jury

In standard civil and criminal litigation, typically there is a judge and a jury. The judge controls the courtroom, and the jury decides the facts and verdict. But in family court systems, USUALLY the judge is also the jury. This means that the individual differences of a judge are in play as opposed to a more diverse jury. Though these problems do exist within typical criminal juries in family court all a party has to do is convince one person.

2. Possible to not involve attorneys

It is not uncommon for these court sessions to exclude attorneys, leaving parties to represent themselves.

Reasons
  • Civil Matter: Due to the nature of family courts being civil, attorneys are not guaranteed and are costly.
  • Cost: Attorneys aren't cheap; in a perfect world, there would be little reason to include an attorney. This is what invites absurd and non-fact-driven claims to pass through prosecution defense.
  • Ineligible for public appointment Due to limited resources attorneys are not always provided however they can be but due to limited resources for the family courts its common for parties to not qualify for a provided attorney.
